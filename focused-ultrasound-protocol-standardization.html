Why Wearable Focused Ultrasound Needs Standardized Protocols

Meaningful device comparisons start with comparable protocols. A practical path toward reproducible clinical research evidence.

By Openwater · Published September 17, 2026

A wearable focused ultrasound device is only one part of an intervention. To compare devices meaningfully across trials, researchers need standardized protocol definitions, transparent exposure reporting, and sufficiently aligned study designs. Otherwise, an apparent device difference may reflect how, where, or when stimulation was delivered.

The practical rule: establish what was delivered, to which target, under which controls, before comparing outcomes. Standardization makes a comparison interpretable; it does not, by itself, prove that a treatment works.

This post concerns low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU) for neuromodulation research, also described as transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) or transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS). It is research-methods guidance, not a treatment protocol.

Trial results are not device rankings

Consider two hypothetical trials using wearable devices. One uses a single session and an immediate physiological endpoint. The other uses repeated sessions and a symptom score weeks later. Even if both report a positive result, their effect sizes do not establish which device performs better. The interventions, populations, and questions differ.

Even matching a device's nominal frequency is insufficient. The ITRUSST reporting consensus calls for descriptions of the transducer and drive electronics, drive settings, measured acoustic output, pulse timing, estimated exposure in the brain, and intensity definitions. A measurement in water and an estimate after skull transmission are different quantities; their methods and uncertainty must be identified. [1]

Our conclusion is therefore specific: uncontrolled cross-trial outcome comparisons cannot isolate device performance. Existing studies still inform feasibility, hypotheses, and evidence synthesis when their differences are acknowledged. A direct device comparison needs a design that holds relevant conditions comparable or explicitly accounts for them.

Standardize the description, then align the comparison

Standardization does not require every research question to use one universal waveform. It requires shared definitions, complete methods, and a declared basis for comparison. The acoustic reporting foundation comes from ITRUSST; the broader study-design checklist below is Openwater's practical synthesis. [1] [2]

LayerWhat a comparison needs
Acoustic exposureCalibration method, carrier frequency, pressure and intensity definitions, measurement medium, focal dimensions, and estimated in-brain exposure with uncertainty.
Timing and schedulePulse duration, repetition interval, duty-cycle definition, train structure, total exposure, session count, and spacing.
Target and placementAnatomical target, localization method, skull-model assumptions, coupling checks, and criteria for acceptable placement and movement.
Controls and populationEligibility, concomitant interventions, randomization, sham design, sensory masking, and assessment of blinding.
Outcomes and analysisPrespecified endpoints, measurement times, analysis population, missing-data rules, and adverse-event follow-up.
TraceabilityHardware, firmware, and software versions; protocol revision; session records; deviations; and analysis code.

For wearable systems, a repeatable fitting procedure belongs in the methods. Record how placement and coupling are checked and how movement or an interrupted session is handled. The ITRUSST practical guide treats positioning, experimental procedures, and confounds as integral to study quality. [2]

A reproducible signal still needs a valid explanation

Controls can change what a result means. In a multicenter human study, Kop and colleagues showed that auditory confounds could account for motor excitability changes in the online TUS paradigms they tested. That finding does not invalidate all ultrasound effects; it demonstrates why a sham condition and sensory controls must be evaluated for the specific experiment. [3]

Safety evidence also needs its own record. ITRUSST's biophysical safety consensus addresses mechanical and thermal risk under specified conditions. A protocol falling within a consensus framework does not establish clinical efficacy, regulatory authorization, or safety for every population and exposure schedule. Report the assessment assumptions and observed adverse events alongside outcomes. [4]

Open-LIFU: a practical guide to evidence others can inspect

Openwater's approach is to earn trust through an inspectable chain from methods to implementation. Use Open-LIFU's public documentation as a guide to the questions a reproducible study must answer, and assess the evidence for each step.

  • Define the reusable protocol. The released protocol documentation distinguishes a Protocol from a target-specific Solution. It documents pulse and sequence settings, simulation configuration, target constraints, and solution-analysis limits. Record both the protocol revision and the solution used for each session. [5]
  • Document planning and localization. The Slicer workflow describes MRI-linked target planning and photogrammetric co-registration. Localization is one part of the evidence chain; it does not independently verify the acoustic field in the brain or neural target engagement. [6]
  • Verify acoustic output. The water-tank guide documents acoustic testing with calibrated hydrophones. Keep calibration and test records with the study methods; a tank measurement alone does not establish exposure through an individual skull. [7]
  • Make implementation reviewable. Link the exact releases or commits used from the Open-LIFU Python repository and associated software, so another team can inspect the same implementation.

The limits matter. The current protocol guide describes Direct beamforming and uniform segmentation options; it should not be read as proof of a validated, turnkey CT-based skull-correction workflow. The localization workflow does not claim continuous real-time motion tracking. [5] [6] The published localization-study overview is explicitly a mannequin-based bench evaluation awaiting release, and does not establish human targeting accuracy. [8]

Transparent tools support reproducibility; independent replication and well-designed studies establish the strength of the evidence. Open-LIFU is a research platform, not an independently certified clinical-evidence standard.

Start the next study with a reproducibility package

We recommend assembling this package before data collection, then updating it with the study's actual execution:

  1. A versioned protocol and prespecified analysis plan.
  2. Calibration records, exposure estimates, and the assumptions behind them.
  3. Targeting, fitting, coupling, and session-quality procedures.
  4. Control conditions, blinding checks, and outcome definitions.
  5. De-identified session records, deviations, adverse-event reporting, and shareable analysis materials, subject to consent and data governance.

Give each site the same definitions and acceptance criteria. Document justified adaptations. That makes replication testable and helps distinguish a device difference from a protocol difference.
